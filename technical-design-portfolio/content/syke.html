Syke

A 3D Escher-inspired non-linear puzzle game about connection, perspective, and being unable to let go.

Syke is a narrative puzzle experience developed over 9 months by a 31-person student studio at the University of Utah. The game was released on Steam in 2024 to a "new and trending" placement, 8/10 rating, and over 30,000 downloads. As design lead, I focused primarily on building scalable design systems and documentation standards which supported rapid development, cross-disciplinary collaboration, and creative expression across the team. I was directly in charge of 9 designers, and was a central member of the team from the initial prototype through final release. My contributions ranged from onboarding new team members, designing and presenting funding pitches, and organizing design team meetings (even getting the pizza), to prototyping mechanics, establishing documentation standards, and designing a full level level for the final game. Below, I've highlighted three of what I feel are my most most contributions to the final product with concern to the field of technical design.

Design Documentation Standards

Problem

Documentation wasn't being accessed or followed across the studio. Team members struggled to keep information straight, and I was getting comments like "I can't get into HackNPlan again," "Where's the document for this level?", "I forgot we had that feature," and, most alarmingly, "I don't know what this game is about." Our documentation made sense to experienced designers, but was opaque for most of the team.

Outcome

A low-friction documentation system hosted on Miro which was accessible by anyone on the team with internet access. The Design Miro tracked mechanics, blockers, and level designs with rigorous standards. Praised by one engineer as "the best design documentation I've ever seen," Syke's documentation system allowed for cross-disciplinary alignment and catered to multiple information retention styles.

Process

  1. Move to Miro: The team was already using Miro for organizational purposes. Moving design documentation off of HackNPlan and onto a familiar platform was a natural first step, and dramatically improved visibility.
  2. Centralized brainstorming: Encouraged all ideation on Miro so every idea lived in one place and could be easily referenced. This allowed me to track ownership of concepts and maintain a living record of design thinking.
  3. Multi-modal documentation standards: Applied VARK learning modalities to enforce documentation consistency. Designers were insructed to present work vocally in meetings, create mechanics flowcharts, draw bubble maps for level layouts, and write summaries. This ensured visual, auditory, reading-based, and kinesthetic learners could accurately interpret design intent.
  4. Mechanics consolidation: Conducted 1-on-1s with each designer to understand their objectives. Worked with a small engineering/design team to identify repeating patterns and consolidate mechanics into modular, reusable functions.
  5. Implementation graphs: Documented all consolidated mechanics in one enormous visual graph showing how each function connected and interdepended, making implementation straightforward for engineering.
  6. Sprint-based tracking: Created standardized blocker and deliverable trackers for each sprint, giving the team visibility into dependencies and potential roadblocks before they caused problems.
  7. Weekly updates: Revisited and updated all documentation every week to keep it aligned with current progress, preventing it from becoming stale or misleading.

Solution

A consolidated design "bulletin board" in Miro containing all thinking, examples, flowcharts, mechanics graphs, and tracking organized visually for easy consumption by artists, engineers, and designers. The system was accessible, multi-modal, and maintained in real-time.

Impact

  • Significantly improved cross-disciplinary communication and reduced miscommunication
  • Dramatically reduced engineering lift by consolidating 30+ unique mechanics into modular functions
  • Enabled rapid pivots and changes while maintaining consistency and team alignment

Learnings

Establish documentation standards early, but do it collaboratively, never "from on high." Work with the team to figure out what works for them, then enforce it together. Also, good documentation is worth the investment. Late in development, we were nearly forced to cut an entire level, but were able to complete it confidently because of clear, early documentation.

The Forb Hub Redesign

Problem

Level design intention was being lost on, and at worst, confusing our players. Syke's levels are built on a "hub and spoke" model. Each spoke is a linear experience branching from a non-linear hub. Originally, "The Library" served as our hub, but its design was mechanically linear and visually rigid. Meanwhile, "The Forb" was visually striking and distinctly non-linear, but served as one of three spokes. Designers were frustrated, and playtester comments included "Why is one level so round when the others are so square?" and "I don't see why this is the hub."

Outcome

Transitioned The Forb to a central hub role, and moved The Library to a spoke. This one change unified the game's visual identity and made the level relationships intuitive. The Forb eventually became so iconic that it directly influenced the studio's cultural identity.

Process

  1. Resource assessment: Evaluated what was feasible. Level designs were underway but narrative hadn't yet been written for the hub sequences. This meant that mechanics could be reworked to fit a new narrative structure, but ground-up redesigns were unfeasible with our limited production resources.
  2. Address Designer Strengths: The designer for the Library was showing incredible skill with their linear designs, but wasn;t invested in developing a non-linear hub experience. Meanwhile, the designer for the Forb was creating fantastical and non-linear puzzles which didn't suit the linear design necessary for a spoke level.
  3. Structural redesign: The original access logic had players going into books to find levels. By swapping hub and spoke, we could ground the navigation experience in a surreal space, where players enter buildings that house each level.
  4. Design for Visual Metaphor: Players weren't being adequately prepared by the library hub for the surreal experiences of the Forb Spoke. By inverting this relationship, the player would first engage with the surreal Forb hub, and then return to more grounded and familiar spaces in each spoke.
  5. Team pitch and playtesting: I presented the idea to the design team and we immediately playtested rough versions. Positive feedback came quickly, validating the direction.

Solution

The Forb became the hub, and the The Library became a spoke. At the time, this change was extremely controversial, but it feels obvious in retrospect. Level relationships became intuitive, and The Forb brought such visual distinction to the game that it defined the marketing, branding, and narrative moving forwards.

Impact

  • The Forb became the leading visual identity of Syke
  • Level relationships became immediately intuitive to players
  • Designers were creatively enabled to pursue their interests

Learnings

Be ready to adapt and work with the team to implement changes. That said, when making major decisions that affect others' work, extended consultation and explanation of the decision-making process is necessary. One designer expressed resentment about the change later - better communication upfront could have mitigated that.

The School Level Design

Problem

A level designer had to leave the project for health reasons, mid-development, leaving "The School" incomplete. The blockout and narrative intent were documented, but the level needed hands-on leadership to guide the multidisciplinary team through completion. An initial "firefighting team" was assigned but lacked direction and clear goals.

Outcome

The School became one of the game's standout levels, with players praising it as "the best level in the game" and expressing wonder at how "surreal it feels." The level shipped ahead of schedule, with strong visual and gameplay polish.

Process

Completing the level required restructuring the team and approach entirely:

  1. Reorganize for clarity: The initial firefighting team lacked direction without a clear leader and prescribed goals. I reassigned them to opening/ending sections (which required their multidisciplinary talents) and took ownership of The School with one dedicated artist and two engineers.
  2. Modular subdivision: Split the level into sections based on existing documentation. This allowed one discipline to work on one section while others progressed in a parallel "assembly line" style development.

    Early ideation

  3. Ruthless daily playtesting: Completed each room and immediately tested it, ripping out anything that wasn't working. Tests often yielded over a page of hand-written notes, and I ran multiple playtests each week to catch problems early.
  4. Spatial metaphor through narrative collaboration: Worked alongside narrative to sculpt the level using spatial metaphor. The school needed to feel anxious, confusing, and warm to parallel a story of young love.
  5. BP Optimization: By working directly in blueprints, in addition to level design work, I brought our portals mechanic to a steady FPS for use everywhere in the game. Clever culling strategies and minimizing 3D rendering helped speed up performance and improve visuals.

  6. Iteration until polish: Continued iteration until playtest notes dropped below a paragraph per session. At that point, I felt confident calling it "done" and releasing the team to work on other priorities.

Solution

A small, focused team was able to rapidly complete the school level by splitting it into modular sections for parallel work. Aggressive playtesting caught problems early, and clear documentation from the original designer made it possible to maintain creative intent while adapting to new constraints.

Impact

  • Level shipped on time with high quality and polish
  • Players consistently cited it as a standout experience

Learnings

Good early documentation saved this level. Without clear intent and mechanics breakdown, it would have been impossible to justify continuing development. Develop-in-parallel can be absolutely ideal on a smooth team with clear communication. Clear ownership and objectives are essential - despite strong technical abilities, the "firefighting team" failed because I didn't perscribe accurate goals.
