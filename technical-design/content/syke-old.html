What is Syke?

Syke ​is a 3D Escher-inspired non-linear puzzle game. This was my capstone project at the University of Utah, and my most substantial project to date. Built in just nine months by a 31-person studio, Syke is emotionally dense, beautiful, and atmospheric, with a three-hour playtime.

As design lead, it was my job to focus designers on goals which upheld our core pillars. That meant building modular gameplay systems and creating documentation standards, in addition to getting hands-on with level design and playtesting.


I can confidently say that Syke succeeded because I helped build systems that supported creativity, iteration, and readability at every level of production.

Documentation

This is an example of the depdency trackers I made for each sprint of Syke's development. By visualizing any known dependencies ahead of time, the design team stayed flexible enough to change course before running into blockers.


Knowing that student developers will only reference documentation they can access easily, I used a single Miro board for all design touchpoints. To improve clarity, I incorporated the VARK learning modalities into our documentation standards.

By requiring visual documentation in the form of diagrams on top of any written documentation, as well as oral presentation in meetings, I helped maintain documentation which could accommodate to vocal, aural, reading, and kinetic learners alike.

Design Leadership

I was also responsible for planning and executing several key design decisions, including one key level design pivot.


After initial blockouts were complete, Syke had three relatively conventional environments and one radically unique design - a forest laid inside of an orb, with a grand tree bridging the center. "The Forb" was one of the spokes of our "hub and spoke" map, and feedback indicated confusion about this level's significance. At the same time, the level designer for the "Library Hub" doing incredible work creating a linear player experience, but struggling to design a strong hub.

On top of this, implementation was proving more difficult than expected, and maintaining polish across increasingly large levels was becoming unrealistic. It was clear to me that something had to change.


I proposed to the team that we make The Forb our new hub, and move the library to a spoke. This was controversial, since neither level was designed with this intent, but tester feedback was overwhelmingly positive and The Forb quickly became an anchor for Syke's identity.

Systems Design

One of Syke's top objectives was to capture the "First-Person, Atmospheric Puzzle/Exploration" and "Escape Room" tags on Steam. At the time of our pitch, only 44 other games captured both. In particular, we were aiming for the tactile feelings of exploring an escape room, like pressing butttons on a keypad or searching an object for clues.


My systems work focused on building modular mechanics that helped level designers prototype quickly and engineers implement efficiently. Starting from gameplay pillars and narrative guides, I had level designers sketch out any mechanics or interactions they needed, and using these sketches, I worked between engineering and design to author the systems diagram included on this page.

Programming

Througout development, I was running support on implementation to make sure level designers were equipped to continue their work. This included daily level walkthroughs to make sure nothing was falling behind or broken, as well as VFX and Blueprint programming. Some of my VFX work is included here: I noticed that players weren't responding to the "gravity flip" pads, so I built a Niagara particle emitter and tuned it to designer specifications.

The portals we were using were extremely non-performant and had buggy detection code. It took a lot of late nights, but I rebuilt the portals to remove a nasty tick operations and cull when out of view. This effort saved us up to 30fps in affected levels.

This was a test of "perspective mechanics" which were eventually cut in lieu of puzzles which required active player interaction.

Level Design

Halfway into development, a level designer had to exit the project. After many discussions, I took on the responsibility of finishing "The School."

The basic blockout and narrative for the level was already under production: Within three classrooms, navigational puzzles mirrored tests, confessions, and the quaint anxieties of first loves. These three rooms ended up serving multiple purposes, and splitting the level up into sections meant art could step in on a room-by-room basis and perform simultaneous implementation.

A school setting often implies anxiety, but this one needed to feel comfy and bright by the end. I focused on themes of dawn, childhood, and repetition to communicate a sense of growing friendship.

Narratively, "The Chemistry Room" is where our characters start their romance. I focused on spatial metaphor to convey the emotional themes:


As the player enters the room, their world is quite literally turned "upside down," and they are confronted by an exam table with a spotlight pointed at it.

As the player transitions to the wall, they encounter more desks. This space is uncluttered: The characters are focused as they perservere through their anxiety.

Finally, the room resolves in the upright position. It's chaotic and messy, but there's a sense of life and belonging that wasn't here to begin with. The afternoon sun is glowing through the window and even the spotlight that seemed so singular is revealed to be just one of many.
